American Voices — Story

Literary Fiction

The afternoon mail carrier had been coming to their block for nineteen years, and by 2019 she knew which houses held yapping dogs before the screen door opened, which mailboxes stuck, and which porches had been repainted since last fall. She also knew that the man in the blue house had stopped collecting his mail sometime in the spring. Not altogether — the bank statements still vanished, the coupons still arrived — but the magazines and the occasional parcel had started piling up behind the screen. By August, the pile had formed a small city on the porch steps.

She mentioned it to her supervisor once, a kind of question, and was told that some people just lost interest in things. That was that.

It was September when the daughter’s car pulled into the driveway — a woman in her forties driving a rental with Minnesota plates. She had been living in Minneapolis for the better part of a decade, far enough away that holidays had become shorthand for obligation. She stood on the porch and looked at the mail pile with the detached clarity of someone who had been driving for nine hours and had not yet arrived anywhere in particular.

Inside, the house smelled like lemon oil and something else — something mineral and quiet, like a library that has forgotten to be used. Her father was sitting at the kitchen table with a cup of tea that had gone cold, and he looked up at her with an expression she would spend the next two years trying to describe to people who asked. Not confusion, exactly. More like the look of someone watching a movie they had already seen but couldn’t remember the ending to.

They didn’t talk about what had happened to the mail. That felt too small and too large at the same time. Instead she walked through the rooms, touching surfaces, looking at the things that had become the record of a life she had been living at a distance: the books he had stopped reading halfway through, the television schedule he had stopped following entirely, the garden that had become a conversation between weeds and a watering can that occasionally remembered its purpose.

In the bedroom closet, on the top shelf behind a hat box that hadn’t been opened since her mother’s death, she found a shoebox containing seven years of letters. Not sent letters — received ones. Letters from people whose names she didn’t recognize, written in handwriting she had never seen, each one a few pages long and carefully folded. Seven years of correspondence her father had never mentioned, organized by date, bound with a rubber band that had gone brittle and broke when she touched it.

She brought the box to the kitchen table and set it beside the cold tea.

He looked at the box and then at her, and something moved across his face that she would later identify, in a moment alone in the bathroom, as the specific kind of grief that comes from being understood too well by the wrong person at the wrong time.

“You found them,” he said.

“I found them.”

These were letters from people he had met only once, or thought he had met, at conferences and diners and bus stations during the years he traveled for work — years that corresponded, she realized as she read, to exactly the period in her childhood when he was most often absent from the house. Each person had written to him about something they had discussed in a single conversation — a book, a decision, a life change — and he had, it appeared, written back. And they had written back again. And again. For years, across states, across silences, across the particular strangeness of caring about what happened to a stranger.

He had been, she slowly understood, a kind of unintentional counselor. People had told him things in airport bars and conference hallways, and he had listened in a way that apparently made them feel heard, and then they had written to him — long letters about their marriages, their faith, their children who had stopped speaking to them, their quiet failures that no one else knew about — and he had answered every one. Not with advice, as far as she could tell. With acknowledgment. With the kind of response that says: I remember what you told me, and it mattered.

She read a letter from a woman in Tucson whose husband had died and who had, on a flight connection in Denver twelve years ago, ended up sitting next to him and had told him about it — the death, and everything behind the death — and he had listened with an attention that she said in the letter had made her feel, for the first time in months, like a person rather than a problem. She had written to thank him, and he had written back, and they had kept going.

Her father sat and drank his cold tea and let her read.

She asked him why he had never told her, and he looked at her in a way that was no longer the unclear look from the porch. It was sharp and certain and a little sad.

“Because you were my important conversation,” he said. “The others were something else. I didn’t know how to explain that it was both.”

That night she drove to a diner off the interstate and sat in a booth and ordered coffee and called her own daughter in Minneapolis and told her — badly, in fits and starts, working around the hardest parts — about the letters, about the mail pile, about the man who had spent seven years writing to strangers in airport layovers. Her daughter listened and then asked her a question that was both a question and an accusation and a kind of love, and the answer was long, and the coffee went cold again, and outside a mail carrier’s truck went by on the interstate, headed somewhere it had been many times before.
